## Checkpoint 1 (15 minutes)

### Question 1

Why does the 50-character guideline exist, specifically? What breaks when subjects are too long?

### Your Response

> Primarily, we keep commit subject lines to 50 characters or less to allow the `git log --oneline` command to display properly without ellipses or cutting off potentially important information. Effective commits should be concise otherwise it may indicate the commit is doing too much and needs to be split.

### Professor Feedback

You nailed the core point: `git log --oneline` truncation is the primary driver, and a subject that needs 80 characters to describe itself is often a signal the commit is doing too much. Both of those are right.

What you didn't mention: the 50-character target isn't the only threshold in play. The lesson establishes *two* numbers — 50 as the target, 72 as the hard cap. The gap matters because `git log --oneline` truncates around 72 in most terminals, and GitHub's UI does the same. So the rule is: aim for 50 (discipline), never exceed 72 (technical constraint). At 51 characters you're not catastrophically broken — at 80 you're definitely truncated somewhere.

Also worth holding on to: display contexts beyond `git log` include GitHub's PR commit list, the blame view, and commit history in VS Code's Source Control panel. The 72-character ceiling applies everywhere Git surfaces commit subjects.

## Appendix: Other Exchanges

### Exchange 1: Capitalization Convention Pushback

**Your challenge:**

> I'm pushing back on the capitalization of the first letter of the description. Every source I have read on the topic shows all lowercase, with some sources saying "Good convention does not make a good commit" — meaning it's not probably not that big of a deal...

**Professor Response:**

You're right, and I'll own this: I flagged it too confidently. The Conventional Commits specification itself uses lowercase descriptions in its examples — `feat(api): send an email to the customer when a product is shipped` — and the projects that originated CC (Angular, for instance) consistently use lowercase. The capitalization rule comes from Chris Beams' "7 Rules" article, which predates CC and treats the subject line as a standalone sentence rather than a prefixed format.

When you combine `feat(tasks):` with a capitalized description, you end up with mixed case that's arguably *less* clean, not more. Many teams that adopt CC land on all-lowercase descriptions precisely for that reason.

The honest answer is: capitalization after the CC prefix is a project-level style choice, not a spec requirement. What *is* non-negotiable from the spec: imperative mood, 50/72, no trailing period, and a meaningful description. Capitalization is in the "pick a convention and be consistent" bucket.

Your original answer — `feat(tasks): add mark complete checkbox` — was valid. The lesson material has been corrected accordingly.

Good catch. Pushing back on rules you've read conflicting things about is exactly the right instinct — and this one genuinely warranted it.

### Exchange 2: Hands-On Rewrite Exercise

**The five bad commits:**

```
7f2a19c fixed the bug
c4e830b updates to calendar
1a9b22d added stuff for tasks
e52d7f0 working on google calendar integration.
b8f1c3a wip
```

**Your rewrites:**

> 1. `fix(api): obscure token visibility in code`
> 2. `feat(calendar): add '4-day' view`
> 3. `feat(tasks): add task types`
> 4. `feat(calendar): integrate Google Calendar`
> 5. *This should not be a commit at all. Combine with next commit or wait until enough work is done to create a commit.*

**Professor Feedback:**

**#1 — `fix(api): obscure token visibility in code`**

Correct type and mood. The description "obscure token visibility in code" is slightly awkward — "obscure" as a verb isn't the clearest choice here; "mask" or "hide" would land more immediately (`fix(api): mask API token in debug output`). The bigger point: you had to *invent* a specific bug because the original gave you nothing to work with. That's the real lesson — `fixed the bug` is so vague it's unrecoverable. You can't rewrite it accurately without reading the diff. Good instinct to pick something plausible.

**#2 — `feat(calendar): add '4-day' view`**

Clean, specific, right type, imperative mood. The quotes around `4-day` are a mild style choice but not wrong. Works well.

**#3 — `feat(tasks): add task types`**

Short, specific, correct. Nothing to add.

**#4 — `feat(calendar): integrate Google Calendar`**

Best mechanical fix of the five. You caught the trailing period, eliminated the present progressive, and landed on a tight, informative subject. "integrate Google Calendar" is clear enough that you know exactly what the commit is about without opening it.

**#5 — `wip` → should not be a commit at all**

This is the right answer, and it's more sophisticated than just rewriting the subject line. A WIP commit has no place in a shared branch's history — it either gets amended into the next meaningful commit, squashed before a PR, or shouldn't exist yet. Recognizing that some "bad commits" are structurally wrong — not just badly worded — is the deeper skill here.

Lowercase throughout, consistent with the CC spec. Correct call given the capitalization discussion.
